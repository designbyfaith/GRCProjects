# Reflection: Project 05

> Author: Faith Olofintuyi

## 1. What was hardest about writing in plain language?
Dropping legal jargon. As an attorney, I think in statute numbers and defined terms, but a CFO reading a risk acceptance needs to know what it means for the business. I kept citations like CCPA §1798.150 and Penal Code §632 in the compliance table, where an auditor or lawyer would look for them. In the body, I explained them plainly: the law sets damages of $107 to $799 per person, but only for certain kinds of data; and in California, everyone has to agree to a meeting being recorded. The test I used was whether a reader could act on a sentence without looking anything up. Plain language also helped me stay neutral: when every side can follow the reasoning, no one feels the document was written against them.

## 2. How did you decide on the dollar impact estimates?
I built them up from the scenario's own numbers: invoice sizes for diverted payments, client concentration for revenue at risk, daily AP costs for disruption, and CCPA's statutory range. I kept the ranges wide on purpose, because the honest answer is that no one knows which scenario would happen. Then I checked the math. My first draft put the worst case at $37M, but the impact table only added up to about $22M, so I corrected it. A number that doesn't add up undermines everything else in the document. Because I was the neutral fact-finder, I was careful not to stretch the figures to make my own recommendation look stronger.

## 3. What would you do if the CFO refused all options and demanded acceptance?
Document it and escalate. I would record the decision as Option C, with the same honest description of the risk. I would also check whether the CFO has the authority to accept it, because the most likely losses exceed $1M, and that may be above what one executive can approve alone. Finally, I would make sure the Audit Committee is told. My job is not to block the business. It is to make sure the right people make the decision knowingly and that the record shows it. This is close to what actually happened here: the executives turned down my recommendation, and the job was to document their choice as carefully as my own.

## 4. How would you feel presenting this if the breach happened next month?
Confident. The document shows the risk was identified, explained in plain terms, and priced. It shows the Risk & Compliance team recommended Option B, and the executive team chose Option D for stated reasons. It also shows the conditions attached to that choice. If a breach happened, I could walk anyone through what we knew, what we advised, and what was decided. The decision belonged to the business, and the record makes that clear. Losing the recommendation was disappointing, but I learned that documenting the decision well matters more than winning the argument.

## 5. What's the difference between this document and a "cover your ass" exercise?
It has teeth. A CYA memo records a warning and stops there. This document attaches conditions, an expiry date, named owners, and deadlines to the acceptance, and it lapses automatically if they are not met. It also sets triggers that force a new decision, such as a suspicious bank-change request or an auditor finding. The goal is not to protect the Risk & Compliance team. It is to make sure the business follows through on the choice it made. It also treats every side fairly: Finance's cost concerns and Engineering's capacity limits are recorded as real reasons, not excuses.
